Your five questions

Every question you asked, answered only as far as the sources go. Where the sources stop, the answer stops.

Q1. What is a reasonable and normal increase under local, state and federal law for 94122?

In San Francisco the ceiling is not a market judgement. It is the Rent Board's published annual percentage applied once every 12 months on the anniversary date to base rent, plus any annual increases the owner skipped and is still entitled to bank. For an increase effective between 1 March 2026 and 28 February 2027 that percentage is 1.6%. State law adds nothing for a covered SF unit, because AB 1482 excludes housing that is already under a stricter local cap. Federal law sets no rent ceiling at all.

Unit 1: 1.6% = $63.92, or 3.0% = $119.85 if the 1.4% is banked. Unit 2: 1.6% = $50.40, 11.5% banked = $362.25, or 13.1% = $412.65 combined.

Q2. How do large real-estate corporations handle increases, and how do individual owners?

The obligations are the same for both: the same 1.6%, the same licence test, the same notice rules, the same ban on algorithmic rent-setting, and the same exposure to a tenant petition. The ordinance turns on the unit being covered, not on who owns it or how many units they hold. What differs by law is only the narrow federal 'Mrs Murphy' exemption for an owner-occupied building of four or fewer families, which an institutional owner can never use and which does not apply here either.

Q3. What is the correct way to inform tenants, and what are reasonable reasons for an increase?

Correct way: a written notice served 30 days ahead (90 days if the total exceeds 10%, plus 5 days if mailed) that states the dollar amount, the percentage, the effective date and which portion is annual versus banked, with the licence already current on the effective date. Lawful reasons are documentary rather than rhetorical: the published annual adjustment; properly banked prior years; a certified capital improvement; a documented operating-and-maintenance petition (capped at 7% in any 12 months); a certified utility or bond passthrough.

Q4. Would the increase survive review by a San Francisco tenant lawyer?

It can. The arithmetic is checkable and the sources are published. Nine conditions have to be true at once: covered unit; licence current on the effective date; at most 12 months since the last increase; anniversary date correct; base rent correct; percentages read from form 571 and added not compounded; notice content complete; notice period correct for the size of the increase; and no retaliation or protected-class motive. On the facts supplied the arithmetic passes; the licence status and two rent-history facts cannot be confirmed and are flagged.

Q5. Is it OK to raise the rent on one unit only, or should both go up to avoid a discrimination claim?

Raising one unit and not the other is lawful. Discrimination law asks whether the reason for a difference is a protected characteristic, not whether the numbers match: the federal and state statutes list the protected classes, and none of them is 'has a below-market rent' or 'has lived there longer'. Matching percentages is not a defence either — applying 13.1% to Unit 1 would simply be unlawful. The consistent, defensible approach is one written rule applied to each tenancy's own lawful ceiling. If identical treatment matters as a policy choice, the lawful route is to impose the annual 1.6% on both and record in writing that banked increases are being deferred, not waived — deferring keeps them banked.

Identical-percentage option: 1.6% on both = $63.92 for Unit 1 and $50.40 for Unit 2, with the unused balances retained as banked amounts.
