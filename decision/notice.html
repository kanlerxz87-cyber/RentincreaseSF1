Notice & reasons

30 days or 90 days — and no, you do not need a reason

For an annual or banked increase on a rent-controlled unit, San Francisco does not require the owner to justify the increase. What it requires is a licence, a correct calculation and a conforming notice.

The 180-day retaliation trap

Under Civil Code §1942.5(a), if a tenant has (in good faith) given a §1942 notice, complained to the owner about tenantability, complained to an appropriate agency, or if there has been an inspection or citation, the owner may not increase the rent for 180 days from the latest of those events, where the tenant is not in default in rent. Subdivision (d) extends the same protection to tenants who have lawfully organised or participated in a tenants' association. A tenant may invoke §1942.5(a) once in any 12-month period.

Operational consequence: before serving any notice, check whether either household has made a habitability complaint, requested repairs in writing, or contacted an agency in the last six months. If so, the timing of the increase is a legal question, not a business one. See the tenant-lawyer test.

Practical drafting notes that cost nothing and remove the obvious attack lines

  • Use the percentage in force on the effective date, and say which period it comes from.
  • Show the arithmetic on the face of the notice: base rent → percentage → dollar amount → new base rent.
  • Itemise the banked years separately, with the year and percentage each.
  • Do not round up; carry the cents exactly.
  • Do not include pass-through amounts in the base used for the percentage.
  • State the service method and keep proof of service dated on the day you serve.
  • Never reference a tenant's protected characteristic, immigration status, complaint history or tenancy-organising activity anywhere in the notice or in any covering conversation.
