Executive summary · live-verified 2026-11-13

Raising rent on two 1940 flats in the Outer Sunset

The rule you read about is real, and it is a San Francisco local law, not a state law. It applies to an individual owner of a two-unit building exactly as it applies to a corporation. Nothing is sent to the Rent Board for approval before you notify a tenant — but the unit must be licensed on the day the increase takes effect.

Is the 2023 Rent Board requirement a state law or a local law?

It is local. The duty sits in the San Francisco Administrative Code, Chapter 37, §37.15 — a City ordinance, titled “Reporting Obligations; Licensing” and footnoted on the City's own page as “Added by Ord. No. 265-20, effective January 18, 2021”. The Rent Board's 2023 press release describes it in exactly those terms: “A new law passed by the City and County of San Francisco.”

Why 2023 is the year you remember. The ordinance took effect in 2021, but it started on a staggered timetable. For buildings with 10 or more residential units the duty began 1 July 2022. For condominiums and buildings with fewer than 10 units — which is what a two-unit 1940 building is — the duty began 1 March 2023, with updates due every 1 March after that. The Rent Board's adopted Rule §13.14 says the same thing: for buildings with fewer than ten units it “shall only apply to rent increases that are effective on or after March 1, 2023.”

So: the licence rule is local (City ordinance); the notice rule you must also obey is state (California Civil Code §827); and the anti-discrimination rules are both state and federal. Three layers, three sources.

I am one individual, not a corporation. Do I still have to report and hold a licence before notifying tenants?

Yes — and this is the part most owners get wrong, so read it twice.

§37.15(a) opens with “Owners of residential dwelling units subject to this Chapter 37…”. There is no individual-owner exemption, no small-landlord exemption and no two-unit exemption anywhere in the section. The Rent Board's own Housing Inventory page says “All owners of residential property are required to report” and adds that most owners must report “even if you are not a landlord.”

But the mechanism is not what the phrase “apply for a licence” suggests. There is no licence application and no approval step. You file the annual Housing Inventory for each unit; the licence for a tenant-occupied unit is then generated automatically. Reporting is free. The Rent Board states plainly: “Landlords are not required to file a petition with the City to impose these types of rent increases” — so an ordinary annual or banked notice goes to the tenant, never to the Board for approval.

The precise timing test (this is the whole ballgame). Adopted Rule §13.14 says the effective date of the increase — not the date you serve the notice — is what decides whether you were licensed. An increase effective while you are unlicensed is “null and void”. Your banking rights survive, so you do not lose the skipped years. Practical consequence: finish the reporting and confirm the licence before you serve, because the safe sequence costs nothing and the wrong one can void the whole increase.

Privacy first: the Housing Inventory submission is public. It can carry the unit address, owner or manager name and business contact, square footage, bed and bath counts and occupancy history. Rent is reported in $250 increments, not exact dollars. See F-18.

The process, step by step — written for someone who has never done this

Before you do anything else: the only thing standing between you and an unenforceable increase is a licence on the effective date. Here is the whole sequence.

  1. Confirm the unit is covered. Rent control generally attaches to residential units built on or before 13 June 1979 that are not exempt. A reported 1940 build points to coverage, but confirm the parcel on the official Property Information Map and, if unsure, ask a Rent Board counsellor (415-252-4600). F-07, F-08.
  2. Rebuild the rent ledger for each unit. You need the tenancy start date, the lawful starting rent, the date and amount of every increase actually imposed, and the years when an allowed increase was skipped. Banking depends entirely on this. F-05.
  3. Work out the anniversary date. The first annual increase can take effect 12 months after the tenancy began; after that you cannot increase again until 12 months have passed, and the effective date becomes the new anniversary date. F-01.
  4. Report into the Housing Inventory at the Rent Board Portal (free, online, 24/7). Report each unit. The licence for a tenant-occupied unit is then generated automatically. Remember the submission is public. F-18.
  5. Confirm both licences are current in the portal before you serve anything, and screenshot the record.
  6. Calculate the amount. Take the percentage in force on the effective date (not the date you serve), add any banked percentages — added, never compounded — apply the total to base rent only, and do not round up.
  7. Draft the notice. It should state the dollar amount, the percentage, the date it takes effect, and which part of it is banked.
  8. Serve it in time. 30 days for an increase at or under 10%; 90 days above 10%; add 5 more days if you mail it.
  9. Re-check licence status on the effective date and keep the proof with the ledger.
  10. Do not touch the rent while any of this is unresolved, and re-check File No. 261020 before any large banked catch-up.
