One unit or both

Neither “one” nor “both” is automatically safe

The statutes prohibit treating someone differently because of a protected characteristic. They do not require you to treat every tenant identically, and identical treatment is not a defence if the selection reason is itself unlawful.

So: one unit or both?

Decide it on the ledgers, not on optics. Under San Francisco's own rules the two units are not similarly situated: Unit B's rent has not moved since 2019 while Unit A's has not moved since 2025, so the banked entitlement diverges sharply. Applying each unit's own lawful maximum is arithmetically explainable and is the single most defensible posture available.

The risk pattern to avoid is not “different numbers” — it is raising the unit occupied by a household with a protected characteristic while leaving an identically situated unit untouched. That is the fact pattern a tenant lawyer looks for, and it is why the reason must be written down before the notice is served, not reconstructed afterwards.

Two cautions specific to this file:

  • Unit B's banked entitlement, if the ledger confirms it, produces an increase above 10% — which triggers the 90-day notice rule and sits above the cap proposed in File No. 261020. Size and timing matter more here than symmetry.
  • If you raise only one unit, write down in one sentence why that unit and not the other, in terms of dates and ledgers only. If you raise both, do the same. The sentence is the evidence.

This site does not promise that any particular choice would survive a lawyer's review. It shows what the statutes prohibit and what the record would need to show.
