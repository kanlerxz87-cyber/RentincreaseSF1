Task T5 · institutional and individual owners

There is no separate "corporate standard" — but there are size-based procedures

The Rent Ordinance regulates "a landlord", not a category of landlord. A REIT with 10,000 Bay Area units and an owner with two flats in the Sunset face the same 1.6%, the same anniversary rule, the same licence and the same notice. What genuinely differs by size is administrative: who reports to the Housing Inventory, in what format, and when.

What the ordinance says about who it binds

"A landlord who has reported the required information about their rental unit to the Rent Board as set forth in Section 37.15 shall have a license to impose annually a rent increase which does not exceed a tenant's base rent by more than 60% of said published increase." — §37.3(a)(1). The same section governs banking, additional occupants and passthroughs without regard to the size of the owner's portfolio.

The algorithmic-pricing ban is the clearest statement of what San Francisco considers normal. Section 37.10C makes it unlawful to supply or use revenue-management software that sets or recommends rents using non-public competitor data, with civil penalties of up to $1,000 per unit per month of violation and attorney fees for a prevailing tenant or tenant non-profit (S7). In other words, the City has legislated against exactly the practice that large operators are best equipped to run. "Normal business practice" in San Francisco therefore means a documented, per-tenancy calculation — not a software output.

What "normal business practice" defensibly means for these two units

The defensible version

  • One increase per tenancy per 12 months, on the anniversary date.
  • Amount equal to the published allowance, plus any banked allowance itemised by year.
  • Licence current; notice served in time; components stated.
  • Identical method applied to every unit, documented unit by unit, so that the difference between $3,995 and $3,150 is explained by base rent and history rather than by who lives there.
  • No passthroughs bundled, no market-rent language, no pricing software.

The version that draws a petition

  • A round-number increase ("let's go to $4,200") with the percentage reverse-engineered afterwards.
  • An increase timed to a roommate change, a repair request, or a lease renewal that was really a rent review.
  • Banked percentages compounded, or applied without the year-by-year schedule.
  • Different treatment of two similar households that cannot be explained by rent history.
  • Any suggestion — even informal — that the increase is meant to encourage a move.

The second list is not hypothetical risk. Bad-faith pressure to vacate and discrimination are both actionable under §37.10B, with damages of at least three times actual damages or $1,000 statutory, whichever is greater, plus attorney fees (S6). The first list is also precisely what the ordinance prescribes, which is why it is the safest description of "normal business practice" available.
