Executive summary · prepared 6 November 2026

What a lawful — and defensible — rent increase looks like for two 1940 rent-controlled flats in the Outer Sunset

Both units sit in a building constructed in 1940, so both are covered by the San Francisco Residential Rent Stabilization and Arbitration Ordinance (Administrative Code Chapter 37). That single fact decides almost every question in this brief: local law, not state or federal law, sets the ceiling; the Rent Board publishes that ceiling once a year; and every dollar must be traced to a published percentage, a date, and a notice.

Bottom line

  1. The ceiling is local. Because the building was built in 1940, both units are subject to the San Francisco Rent Ordinance's annual allowable increase — 1.6% for increases effective 1 March 2026 through 28 February 2027. The statewide cap (AB 1482, currently 8.8% in San Francisco) does not apply to units that are already under the local rent-increase limits. Law by level →
  2. The "reasonable and normal" number is the published 1.6%. It is the only increase an owner may impose on a sitting tenant without filing a petition, and it is calculated as 60% of the Bay Area CPI change. Anything above it needs a Rent Board petition (capital improvements or operating-and-maintenance costs) and is capped at 10% in any 12 months. Calculations →
  3. Unit 1 should be increased by 1.6% only, effective 1 February 2027. The rent is $3,995.00 and has not moved since the lease began on 1 February 2025. The tenant change in April 2026 was a partial change of occupancy (an original tenant remains), so no vacancy reset and no "additional occupant" charge is permitted. New rent: $4,058.92 after 30-day written notice. Unit detail →
  4. Unit 2's lawful maximum is 13.1%, not 13.1% compounded. Seven annual allowances were never used (1.8 + 0.7 + 2.3 + 3.6 + 1.7 + 1.4 + 1.6). Percentages are added, not compounded, and may not be rounded up, giving $3,562.65. That is a lawful figure, but it is the figure most likely to draw a tenant petition — and it is economically an average of about 1.8% a year, not a 13% jump. Unit 2 detail →
  5. Raising one unit and not the other is lawful. Nothing in Chapter 37, the Fair Housing Act, or the Fair Employment and Housing Act requires parallel treatment; differences in base rent, anniversary date and banked history are lawful reasons for different outcomes. What is unlawful is a difference that is because of a protected characteristic, or an increase imposed in bad faith or in retaliation. Document the objective calculation for each unit and apply the same policy going forward. One unit or both →
  6. Two administrative prerequisites can void an otherwise perfect increase. The owner must hold a current rent-increase licence from the Rent Board Housing Inventory (Administrative Code §37.15), and the notice must state which portion of the increase is annual and which is banked (§37.3(b), Rule 4.10). A defective notice can render the entire increase void. Notice procedure →
