Task T2 · unit records and arithmetic

Units, tenancies and the exact maximum lawful rent

Two flats in the same pre-1979 building. Both are under rent control, both have never had an increase, and both therefore have unused allowances stacked up. What differs is how much history has accumulated — nearly seven years for Unit 2, one year for Unit 1.

Why the April 2026 tenant change does not reset the rent

Two independent provisions block a reset. First, the Rent Ordinance prohibits an increase solely because a tenant has added an occupant, "notwithstanding a rental agreement or lease that specifically permits a rent increase for additional occupants" (§37.3(a)(11)(A)), and the Rent Board repeats the rule in plain language: "A landlord cannot charge additional rent simply because a new (or additional) roommate has moved into a unit with the existing tenant" (S1). Second, the Costa-Hawkins vacancy-reset right is expressly disapplied to "partial changes in occupancy … where one or more of the occupants … remains an occupant in lawful possession" (§37.3(d)(2)(B), S4; Cal. Civ. Code §1954.53(a), S23).

Because Tenant 1A (and 1B) remained, the tenancy is continuing, the base rent stays at $3,995.00, and the anniversary date stays 1 February. Had all original tenants vacated and an entirely new household moved in, the analysis would be different — see flag F2.

Neither figure is rounded up: the Rent Board states increases "cannot be rounded up to the nearest dollar" (S1), and Rule 4.12(a) says banked increases "shall not be compounded and shall not be rounded up" (S9).

Flag F1 — which total is right? Rule 4.12(a) says "a full 12 months must have elapsed from the date that an annual rent increase … could have been imposed before this banking section becomes applicable", which allows the 1.4% to be banked by 1 February 2027. The Rent Board's public explainer page instead says a skipped increase is banked only if "at least 24 full months pass without any rent increase". Until a Rent Board counsellor resolves the difference, the conservative 1.6% figure is the safer notice to serve. Full text of both on the Flagged page.

Why the 2019 rate of 2.6% is not in the table

The first annual increase cannot be imposed until twelve months after the lease began (S1). The tenancy began 1 June 2019, so the earliest date was 1 June 2020 — by which time the 1.8% rate was in effect. The 2.6% allowance for 1 March 2019 – 29 February 2020 was never available for this tenancy. Including it would overstate the lawful maximum by $81.90.

Why $3,562.65 and not $3,585.72

Compounding the same seven rates would produce $3,585.72. The ordinance does not permit that: banked increases "shall not be compounded", and the Rent Board's worked examples add the percentages together before applying them to the base rent (S9, S11). Adding first gives 13.1% × $3,150.00 = $412.65, i.e. $3,562.65. The difference between the two methods is $23.07 a month — small, but it is the kind of error a tenant petition would find.

Under option B, the remaining allowance is preserved: "If only part of the allowable increase is used, the rest is banked" (S10). The dollar value of the remainder would be calculated against the base rent in force when it is later imposed, not against today's rent — see flag F8.

Timing arithmetic for Unit 2. A 90-day notice served on 30 November 2026 takes effect on 28 February 2027 — the last day the 1.6% rate is certain. If the notice is mailed, Civil Code §827 incorporates the five-day extension of Code of Civil Procedure §1013, so the practical last mailing date is 25 November 2026 (S21). An effective date of 1 March 2027 or later would use the 2027–28 rate, which is not yet published — see flag F5.
