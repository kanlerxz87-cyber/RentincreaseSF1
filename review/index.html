Executive Summary

San Francisco, Outer Sunset, ZIP 94122 · Building constructed 1940 (rent-controlled) · Two units · Individual property owner. This summary leads with the most important question — the Rent Board license requirement — and then walks through the allowed increases, the notice process, what is reasonable, and what a tenant lawyer would scrutinise.

Front-and-centre answer: Yes — you do need a rent-increase license from the SF Rent Board, and yes — you must report into the Rent Board Housing Inventory, even as an individual (non-corporate) owner of a small 2-unit building. There is no small-owner exemption. The license is issued automatically when you report that a unit is tenant-occupied in the Rent Board Portal, and it must be in effect on the effective date of the increase. An increase imposed without an active license is null and void even if the percentage math is perfect. (Effective for 1–9 unit buildings since March 1, 2023.)

Headline conclusions

  1. License first, notice second. Report both units in the Rent Board Portal and confirm the license is active before serving any notice. An unlicensed increase is void regardless of arithmetic.
  2. The only "reasonable and normal" increase without a Rent Board petition is the published annual rate. For increases effective through 2/28/2027, that rate is 1.6%.
  3. "Catch up to market" is not a lawful reason for a sitting tenant in a rent-controlled unit, and charging extra rent because of the new occupant in Unit 1 is explicitly prohibited.
  4. You are not required to raise both units, but you must apply a uniform, written, neutral policy to both (e.g., the published annual rate on each anniversary, with year-by-year banking). Differences must come from base rent, anniversary date, and banked history — never from a protected characteristic or protected activity.
  5. The conservative, tenant-lawyer-defensible path is: license confirmed → serve 30-day notice for the 1.6% annual increase on each unit's next anniversary → itemise any banked amounts in a separate worksheet → hold off on the full 13.1% on Unit 2 until you decide whether a large jump is worth the heightened scrutiny (it requires 90-day notice and invites a petition).
