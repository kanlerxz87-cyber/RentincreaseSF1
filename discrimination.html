One Unit or Both

Whether to raise one unit or both, tested against discrimination, retaliation and the arithmetic itself.

Direct answer

Raising one unit and not the other is not, by itself, unlawful. What the law prohibits is a difference that is based on a protected characteristic — race, colour, religion, sex, gender identity or expression, sexual orientation, marital status, national origin, ancestry, familial status, source of income, disability, veteran or military status, or genetic information under state law S19, and race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status or disability under federal law S20. A difference that follows from objective rent facts — a different base rent, a different tenancy start date, a different banking history — is the ordinary output of the formula, not evidence of discrimination.

Recommended: apply the same published percentage to both units and document why the dollar outcomes differ. That is the simplest position to defend, and it also avoids the appearance problem that invites an inquiry in the first place.

That the Rent Board will not hear discrimination, harassment or retaliation claims S13 does not reduce exposure — it moves the dispute to a forum where damages and fees are available.

Three concrete risks if only one unit is increased

  1. The differential needs a written reason, and “we chose to” is a weak one. A one-line per-unit rationale that cites base rent, anniversary and banking history converts an unexplained difference into a documented application of the formula S01 S04.
  2. Timing can look retaliatory even when it is not. An increase served within 180 days of a repair request, agency complaint or organising activity is exposed under Civil Code §1942.5, whatever the arithmetic shows S18.
  3. A free challenge exists. Any tenant can file an unlawful-rent-increase petition or a summary petition based on an invalid notice at no cost S13, so a unit that is not increased this year is still a unit whose neighbour's file will be read closely.
What this page is not. It is not a legal opinion on whether any particular tenant belongs to a protected class, and it deliberately records no such information. The analysis assumes only what the brief supplies: rent, dates and occupancy changes. If any tenant has a characteristic protected under S19 or S20, the safe course is unchanged — identical policy, identical process, documented arithmetic, and no reference to the tenant's circumstances in any communication.
